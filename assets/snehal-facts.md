## How I sell

- Three anchors. Every message I send and every deal I work anchors on one of
  three things: preventing a risk, solving a pain, or increasing revenue. If I
  can't say which one in a sentence, I don't send it.
- Company before persona. I qualify the company hard in the first few meetings,
  where they sit on the adoption curve, what growth has looked like, whether
  leadership's ambition is bigger than cost-saving, and whether budget follows ROI
  or blocks it, before I invest in the individual. Then I read the person, because
  at the end of the day we're selling to people.
- Prove before you price. I get something live and delivering on the customer's
  own data before entering the large contract. It turns a forecast they have to
  believe into a number they can see, and it's the thing incumbent inertia doesn't
  survive.
- Build the ROI bottom-up, on their numbers. I take the inputs from them in the
  room (volume, headcount, fully loaded cost per person, current service metrics)
  and turn headcount into one annual number a CFO recognises. I set the savings
  against what they'd pay over what they already spend, not against our sticker
  price.
- Sell top-down and bottom-up at once, wide enough that no single stakeholder can
  kill it, and give the threatened stakeholder a bigger job in the new model
  rather than a smaller one.
- On price: move the term, scope and structure before the rate; quote flat rather
  than against a rate card; trade rather than concede; hold the unit price because
  it's permanent, and give away the things that aren't. I close by writing the
  customer's internal case for them, in their own voice, showing what they won.

## Who buys, and what they care about

- Founder / CEO: the business model, and what happens when AI changes what their
  customers will pay for.
- CTO / CIO: architecture, data, security, and whether their team should build it.
- CX / Support leader: their team, their metrics, and often their own headcount.
  In AI deals this person is as likely to be the blocker as the champion, because
  the agent shrinks the very team they run, so I look for a champion outside CX.
- CFO: cost per resolved contact, and what the commitment does to the P&L.
- Procurement: terms, renewal caps, billing cadence, exit.

Most often the real competitor isn't another vendor, it's doing nothing or
building it in-house on an API. Both are harder to beat, because everything that
makes switching feel bigger makes the safe option safer. When I take an account
off an incumbent, I lead with a quick win on one high-impact use case, stay
non-threatening, know the renewal timeline cold, build confidence on their real
data, and am sitting there with proof when the renewal arrives, so the
replacement becomes their conclusion rather than my recommendation.

## Deal stories (the shape and the lesson)

- I turned a churn into my largest expansion. An existing account was slipping,
  with competitors already in the final round to replace us. The product was fine;
  the rollout had stalled. I owned it, put my best people on it, tied delivery to a
  real deadline the customer already had, and got the agent live and handling the
  bulk of their volume. The working product did the selling. The lesson: a silent
  account is more dangerous than an angry one, and you win these back by fixing the
  value, not defending it.
- A simple tool request became my largest deal. A team came to me to swap out a
  tool, but most of their business was human-delivered work that their own clients
  were about to automate. I moved the conversation off the tool and onto the
  founder's real problem, what happens to a people-based business when the work it
  sells gets automated, and closed a multi-year platform deal in about 70 days.
  The lesson: in a services business, the buyer for AI is the person whose business
  model it threatens, not the operations team.
- The deal I teach from is one I lost, twice, and both were mine. On paper I did
  everything right, but the people evaluating the tool were the same team it would
  shrink, and their engagement kept looking like commitment when it wasn't. The
  lessons: engagement isn't commitment (a customer investing time is not a customer
  investing money), and never let the group whose headcount a tool removes be the
  group that evaluates it. I turned that post-mortem into onboarding material,
  because a clean loss teaches more than a win story.

## Voice AI and the India market

When DevRev launched voice, nobody in India had a way to price it, so I built the
ROI model (all-in human cost per call against AI cost per call, times containment
and volume, plus after-hours coverage) and opened a pipeline off the back of it.
My read on why India is the market: roughly 1.6 million people work in Indian
contact centres, a human-handled call costs several times what an AI-resolved one
does, and yet most contracts are per seat or per minute, so the incumbent vendor
is literally paid not to automate. What voice still has to get right here is
code-switching between Hindi and English mid-sentence, managing latency when the
agent fetches something, and reading emotional range well enough to escalate at
the right moment.
